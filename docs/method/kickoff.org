#+TITLE: Kickoff: validating the structure with the client

A guide for the first sessions with the client, before any data is collected. It is not a slide deck, it is a guide to the conversation, and the order of the topics can change with the discussion.

The goal of these sessions is to agree on scope, structure, format and conventions before the work starts. Don't start gathering data before the structure is approved: every hour spent collecting information in the wrong shape is an hour lost.

* 1. What we are doing and why

Always first: agree on purpose and expectations.

- Say explicitly that this is reverse engineering, not design: the work starts from nothing and rebuilds from what exists.
- Make clear that the document describes the current state. It gives no direction and contains no recommendations unless agreed otherwise.
- Make the value concrete: missing documentation is an operational risk (turnover, maintenance, onboarding, audits), and the assessment reduces it.
- Ask: who uses this documentation after delivery? Is there anyone who will maintain it? In what format do they need it?

* 2. Scope

Get to a final list of what is in and what is out.

- Present the preliminary list of components, if there is one.
- For each component: in scope, out of scope, or on hold with a reason.
- Decide how to treat third-party systems: documented as black boxes (interface only), or excluded and only referenced.
- Decide which environments to document: production only, or also development and test, which matters when configurations differ.
- Fix a reference date: systems are documented as they run in production on that date, and later changes are outside the assessment.
- Ask: are there areas the client considers sensitive and wants to exclude or limit?

* 3. Structure of the document

The most important decision of the kickoff: approve the document in parts.

Present the six parts and their audiences (see [[file:method.org][the method]]): premises, enterprise architecture, architecture by component, functional specifications, infrastructure inventory, non-functional requirements.

- Explain that it is a single document, organized for selective reading by role.
- If the client wants separate documents, it can be done, but it duplicates shared sections and adds coordination. Recommend a single document with a navigable index.
- Ask: are there internal documentation standards to follow (company templates, mandatory structures)?

* 4. Format and delivery

Remove every ambiguity about the final format before starting.

- Ask explicitly: Word, PDF, or something else?
- For Word or ODT: is there a company template (header, footer, styles, logos)? Get the file.
- For PDF: does it need to be searchable, or accessible (PDF/UA)?
- Explain that the document grows incrementally and that partial versions will be available for review along the way.
- Agree on the review cadence: monthly, or every time a component is complete?
- Ask who signs the final approval, and who reviews each part.

* 5. Access and sources

Find out what is available and plan accordingly.

- Source code: where, how, with which credentials.
- Environments: read-only access to production or test. Which monitoring, logging and APM tools exist?
- People: a technical owner for each component, ideally one per system, with agreed availability (say two hours a week).
- Existing documentation: collect it even if fragmentary or outdated. Old specifications, technical emails, presentations, tickets, everything.
- Ask: are there components whose technical owner is not available? It weighs heavily on effort and quality.

* 6. Conventions and markers

Align expectations on quality and on how certain each piece of information is.

- Present the markers: to validate, finding, approved with a date.
- Explain that the document will be explicit about what is not certain. An honest assessment says "we don't know" where it doesn't, instead of inventing or omitting.
- Explain that findings are observations, not criticism. What to do about them is the client's decision.
- Ask: should findings come as a separate section, or stay inside the documentation?

* 7. Glossary and language

Keep the document from speaking a language the client doesn't understand.

- Propose building the glossary together during the first sessions: every technical or domain term used in the document gets a definition.
- Ask for company terms with a specific meaning (internal names of systems, processes, roles).
- Make clear that the glossary is a living document, updated for the whole project.

* 8. Planning and milestones

In the last session, once everything above is settled.

- Estimate the effort per component once the scope is final.
- Decide the order: the most critical components first, or the ones with the most available owner?
- Set intermediate milestones, for example "Part I complete by X", "first three components of Part II by Y".
- Agree on the approval process: asynchronous review on a shared document, or dedicated sessions?
- Name the main risks: owners not available, access denied, systems too complex to document in the time.

* Questions to bring to the sessions

- How many components are in scope, as a first estimate?
- Is there any earlier documentation, even partial or outdated?
- Does the client's team have the skills to maintain the documentation afterwards?
- Are there external deadlines (an audit, a migration project) that constrain the delivery date?
- Is the document for internal use only, or also for third parties (auditors, partners, regulators)?
